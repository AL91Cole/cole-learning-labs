The Blank Monitor

A fictional teacher's monitor says "No signal", but the tower lights up. Your students are the tech team. They ask a question, follow the clues, change one thing, test it and show how they know it worked.

Grades
Grades 7–8
Time
40-minute class period. About 15 minutes for the investigation itself.
Devices
Built for Chromebook, laptop and tablet browsers, with keyboard or touch. Not yet checked on a managed school Chromebook, so try it on a student device first.
Accounts
None
Play The Blank MonitorOpen the student-mode linkAll lessons

Objective

Students use a step-by-step method to find and fix a problem with a computer. They collect clues, choose one change that a clue supports, test it, check the requester's original task and explain how they know it worked.

Words to use: symptom (what the requester sees), hypothesis (an idea you can test) and signal (the picture information that travels from the computer to the display).

Materials

  • One device for each pair, with a current browser.
  • The lesson link, posted where students can select it. It is in the share box on this page.
  • A board or shared screen for four words: Clue, Change, Result, Next step.
  • Your district-approved place to hand in a .txt file, if you plan to collect reports.
  • Paper or sticky notes for the exit question.
  • Before class: run the lab once on a student device, on the student network.

The requester, the classroom, the computer and the lesson file are all fictional. Students work on a virtual bench. They do not touch real equipment.

Lesson sequence

The steps add up to 40 minutes.

  1. Opening demonstration (5 min) Model the method on an everyday example, not on this ticket. Try a fictional classroom speaker with no sound. Ask aloud: did the sound stop after something changed? What would a sound test show right now? Name one thing you would change and say what you expect to hear. Write Clue, Change, Result and Next step on the board. Do not open or solve The Blank Monitor on the shared screen.
  2. Pair up and open the lab (3 min) Give each pair two roles. The Operator works the bench and says the prediction aloud. The Evidence lead records the clue, the one change and the test result. They swap after the first test, and both must be able to explain the result.
  3. Paired investigation (15 min) Pairs work through "Request & clues" and "Workbench & test". Walk the room with three questions: What did you notice? What does that suggest? How will you check? Remind pairs to swap roles after the first test.
  4. Check the requester's original task (5 min) The pair has already opened Lesson_Practice_File.docx on the virtual monitor in "Workbench & test". The test cannot pass without it. On "Check & explain" they select "Check the original task", write the explanation and select "Finish investigation & open report". A changed bench is not the finish line. The original task is.
  5. Report review (4 min) Each pair opens "My report" and reads the four-line summary: Clue, Change, Result, Next step. They check the report for private details and tick the review box. Then they copy it or download it as a .txt file and hand it in through your district's approved system.
  6. Debrief and exit question (8 min) Ask two or three pairs to read their summary lines aloud. Discuss: which observation separates a display that has power from a display that has a picture? Finish with the exit question.

One learner working alone needs about 15 minutes for the whole investigation. Pairs who talk through each step need a little longer, so this plan allows 20 minutes across steps 3 and 4.

What students see in the lab

The lab has three pages. The page itself lists exactly what remains before a student can move on.

  1. Request & clues. Read the request, ask the fictional requester a question, collect at least two clues and save a first idea.
  2. Workbench & test. Make one change on the virtual bench. When the desktop appears, open Lesson_Practice_File.docx on the virtual monitor. Then select "Test my idea". That single action tests the bench and records the result in the experiment notebook. Write what you changed, what happened and what you learned, then select "Save this attempt".
  3. Check & explain. Select "Check the original task", write the explanation and select "Finish investigation & open report" to close the ticket. A sentence helper is there for students who want a starting point: "I noticed ___, so I tested ___."

"My report" opens a preview. It leads with the four-line summary (Clue, Change, Result, Next step), followed by the full evidence, the experiment record and the action log. The student reviews it, ticks the review box and then chooses to copy it or download a .txt file. Nothing is submitted automatically.

Extension: the alternate ticket

For early finishers or a second day, use the alternate ticket, No Picture After a Presentation. It shows the same symptom with a different cause. It is a transfer task: can the pair apply the method again without assuming the same repair? The eighth-grade troubleshooting pilot pack builds a short plan around it.

Evidence to expect

  • In the room. The Evidence lead can point to a recorded clue before the Operator changes anything. The pair makes one change, then tests. Roles swap after the first test.
  • In the report. A .txt file that leads with a four-line summary: Clue, Change, Result and Next step. The full evidence, the experiment record and the action log follow.
  • In the explanation. A sentence or two in the student's own words that links what they noticed to what they tested. For example, a secure explanation names the specific clue the pair collected and what it suggested, not only that the picture came back.
  • At the door. A spoken or written answer to the exit question.

A closed ticket means the simulation's check passed. It is not a grade, and it does not show who did the thinking. A report is the student's own account and can be edited before it reaches you, so read the reasoning.

Look-fors (a concise rubric)

These judge the quality of reasoning. They do not list the repair.

Look forBeginningDevelopingSecure
ClueRestates the symptom or the request.Names a clue from the bench or the requester, but not what it suggests.Names a specific clue and says what it suggests about the cause.
One changeChanges several things at once, or changes something no clue pointed to.Makes one change, but cannot connect it to a clue.Makes one change, says which clue led to it and predicts what the test should show.
Test and original taskStops when the bench looks different.Opens the lesson file because the page asks, but cannot say why that check matters.Runs the test, then checks the requester's original task and says what that check shows.
ExplanationSays it works, with no evidence.Lists the steps in order without linking clue, change and result.Links clue, change and result in the student's own words and names a sensible next step.

A student may explain aloud to you, or use the device's own district-approved dictation to fill the same boxes. Use the same look-fors either way. The site does not record audio and has no speech input.

Keep any answer key or grading rubric of your own in your school's private teacher resources, not on a page students can open.

Exit question

A different computer also shows no picture. What evidence would you collect before choosing the same repair?

Collect the answer on paper, aloud or in your district's approved system. Listen for evidence first and repair second.

Answer

Unplugged fallback

No devices today? Run the same routine on paper: symptom, clue cards, one change, test. It takes about 20 minutes. This is a paper exercise. Students do not handle a real lamp, plug or power strip.

The fictional request: "The desk lamp in the reading corner will not light. It worked yesterday. Can you help?"

  1. Symptom. Read the request aloud. Each pair writes the symptom in one sentence, with no guess about the cause.
  2. Clue cards. Write the four clues below on cards and place them face down. A pair turns over two cards before choosing a change. Hand over the "recent change" card only if the pair asks the requester what changed.
  3. One change. The pair writes one change and what they expect to see.
  4. Test. You play the bench and say what happens. If the lamp stays dark, the pair turns over another card and tries again.
  5. Check the original task. Ask: can the requester read at the desk now? Each student finishes the sentence "I noticed ___, so I tested ___."
Clue cardWhat the card says
LampThe lamp's own switch is on. The bulb is screwed in firmly.
PowerThe lamp is plugged into a power strip. The light on the power strip is off.
RoomThe ceiling lights work, and so does the computer plugged into the wall next to the desk.
Recent changeThis morning the power strip was moved to make room for a fan.

Teacher note for the test step. In this paper scenario the switch on the power strip was bumped off when the strip was moved. Switching the strip on lights the lamp. Any other single change leaves the lamp dark. Use the same look-fors as the lab.
