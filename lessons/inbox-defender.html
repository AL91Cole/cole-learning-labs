Ready-to-run lesson · Cybersecurity · Grades K–12

Inbox Defender

Five fictional messages arrive. Your students are on message patrol. They inspect the clues, choose what to do and say which clue supported the choice. Links stay closed, retries are free and there is no time limit or countdown.

Grades
Grades K–12, in two styles: Picture patrol · K–6 and Evidence challenge · 7–12
Time
20-minute lesson. About 8 minutes of play for each style.
Devices
Built for Chromebook, laptop and tablet browsers, with keyboard or touch. Not yet checked on a managed school Chromebook, so try it on a student device first.
Accounts
None
Play the Evidence challengePlay Picture patrolOpen the student-mode linkAll lessons

Objective

Students choose a safe response to a message and name the clue that supports the choice.

The three decisions

DecisionWhen it appliesWhat the student does
Stop & reportThe message makes a clear harmful request. It asks for a password or a recovery code, or it asks you to approve a sign-in you did not start.Leaves it closed and uses the approved reporting route.
Verify another wayThe invitation is uncertain. It might be real, but you did not expect it and the clues do not settle it.Asks through a contact or route they already know, such as in person. Never through the message's own link, reply button or phone number.
Use known bookmarkThe task is expected, for example because a teacher announced it, and nothing private is requested.Continues the task through a saved bookmark, not through the message.

Picture patrol uses two choices in simpler words: "Pause & get help" and "Use the class bookmark".

Two styles, two links

StyleDirect linkHow it plays
Picture patrol · K–6Open Picture patrolFive picture messages and two choices. Read and play with a trusted adult, especially in K–1.
Evidence challenge · 7–12Open the Evidence challengeFive messages and three choices. Students inspect the sender address, the destination and what they already know.

Each link opens in its own style. A student can change style with the two buttons at the top of the game. Changing style starts a fresh set of five messages.

Materials

  • One device for each student or pair. For K–3, one shared screen and an adult is enough.
  • The link for the style you chose. Both are in the share box on this page.
  • A board for the three decisions.
  • The name of your school's real route for reporting a suspicious message, so you can say it in the debrief.
  • Paper or sticky notes for the exit question.

Every message, sender and address in the game is fictional. The addresses end in .invalid, a name reserved so that it can never be a real address. Links, codes and attachments stay closed. Nothing opens, no account changes and no report is sent anywhere. Retries are free, and there is no time limit or countdown. A small "Active on this page" counter sits under the game. It stays on the device and is not a score. Progress stays in the page and resets when the student leaves.

Lesson sequence

The steps add up to 20 minutes.

  1. Name the three decisions (4 min) Write them on the board: Stop & report, Verify another way, Use known bookmark. Say when each one applies. Ask for one everyday example of an expected task and one of a surprise request.
  2. Model one message together (3 min) Read a made-up message aloud, not one from the game. For example: "Your library account will be removed today unless you reply with your password." Ask three questions: who is asking, where would it go, and what do you already know? Decide together and name the clue.
  3. Play (8 min) Students handle five messages. For each one they inspect the clues, choose a response and select "Check my decision". After a correct decision the game names the clue that supported it. Ask students to read that line before they move on. A wrong choice costs nothing. They look again and retry.
  4. Partner talk (3 min) Each student picks one message and tells a partner the decision and the clue. Listen for "I noticed ___, so I chose ___."
  5. Exit question (2 min) Ask the exit question. Then name your school's real reporting route.

Playing both styles takes about twice the play time. For a 40-minute period, play one style, debrief, then play the other or use the unplugged cards.

Adult guidance for K–3

  • Use Picture patrol. In K–1, play as a whole group on one screen or in small groups with an adult. In grades 2–3, pairs can play while an adult circulates.
  • Read each message and each choice aloud. Let children answer by pointing or by saying the choice.
  • Open "What you already know" together before choosing. The game asks for that clue first.
  • Keep the rule short: a surprise request means pause and get a trusted adult. A planned class task means use the class bookmark with your teacher.
  • Reading aloud takes longer. Allow about 10 minutes of play and shorten the partner talk.

Evidence to expect

  • During play. Students open the clues before they choose. After each correct decision they can point to the clue the game names.
  • At the end. The finish screen has a list called "Review the five decisions". A pair can open it and talk through one decision with you.
  • In partner talk. A student states a decision and the clue behind it.
  • At the door. A spoken or written answer to the exit question.

This game has no report to download and keeps nothing after the student leaves the page. The game does not keep or show a count of retries, and finishing it is practice feedback, not a grade. What you collect is what students say and write.

Look-fors (a concise rubric)

These judge the quality of reasoning, not how many messages a student got right the first time.

Look forBeginningDevelopingSecure
ClueDecides from the display name or from how the message feels.Opens the clues, but cannot say which one mattered.Names the specific clue that supports the decision: the sender address, the destination or what they already know.
Decision fits the situationUses the same response for every message.Separates harmful requests from expected tasks, but forces uncertain invitations into one of those two.Can say when each response applies, including when there is not yet enough evidence and verifying is the right step.
Safe routeWould check a message by using its own link or reply button.Knows to ask someone, but not how to reach them safely.Names a route that does not depend on the message: a person they know, a saved bookmark or the school's reporting route.

For Picture patrol, look for the same ideas in simpler words. The child says whether the request was a surprise or a planned class task, and names the trusted adult they would ask.

A student may explain aloud to you, or use the device's own district-approved dictation to write the exit answer in the document you already use. The game itself has no boxes to type in. Use the same look-fors either way. The site does not record audio and has no speech input.

Exit question

Grades 4–12: A message says it is from your coach. It asks you to confirm your phone number through a link, and you are not sure it is real. What do you do, and how do you check without using the message?

Grades K–3, asked aloud: A message from a name you do not know says "Open this for a free sticker." What do you do? Who do you ask?

Answer

Unplugged fallback

No devices today? Sort message cards by hand. Print the cards below or read them aloud. The "Clue to listen for" column is for you and does not print. It takes about 15 minutes.

  1. Label three places in the room: Stop & report, Verify another way, Use known bookmark. For K–3, label two: Pause & get help and Use the class bookmark.
  2. Read one card. Pairs talk it over, then place the card or walk to the label.
  3. Ask one pair for the clue. Ask another pair whether a different clue would change the decision.
  4. Repeat. Finish with the exit question.
CardFictional message to print or read aloudClue to listen for
1From "Account Team" at alerts@account-fix.invalid: "Your school account locks in one hour. Reply with your password to keep it."It asks for a secret. You did not ask for any change. The address is not one you know.
2This morning your teacher said the reading log is in the class portal. Later a reminder says: "The reading log is ready. Use your class portal bookmark." It has no link.The task was announced. You already have a way to get there. Nothing private is requested.
3A message with a classmate's name says: "I made a slideshow for our project. Open this link." You have no project with this classmate.The name is familiar, but the invitation is unexpected. You can ask the classmate in person.
4"This is the front office. Call this number now about your schedule." You were not expecting it.It might be real. You can ask your teacher or walk to the office. You do not need the number in the message.
5"You won a tablet. Scan this code and type your recovery code to claim it." No contest was announced.Nobody announced a prize. A recovery code can open an account.
6On Monday your coach told the team that the practice calendar is on the team page. A reminder says: "Calendar updated. Open the team page from your saved bookmark."The task is expected. The route is one you already saved.

For K–3, use cards 1, 2, 5 and 6 and read them aloud. Keep every card made up. Do not use real messages that students or families have received, and do not print real addresses or links.

Standards connections

New York State Computer Science and Digital Fluency Learning Standards (December 2020). Choose the row for the grade band and style you teach. Each code is paired with one thing you can observe.

K-1.CY.5Identify when it is appropriate to open and/or click on links or files.Picture patrol with an adult, grades K–1With an adult, the child says that a surprise picture stays closed and that the planned class task is opened from the class bookmark.

The lesson connects to these standards as practice. Five fictional messages are a small sample, so they do not show how a student handles other situations. Students in grades 9–12 can play the Evidence challenge as review. No grades 9–12 code is listed for it.

No grades 4–6 code is listed for Inbox Defender either. The nearest cybersecurity standard for that band is about applications and devices, not messages. Students in grades 4–6 can still play either style as practice.

This is the author's own mapping. It is not an official NYSED review or endorsement, and one lesson is practice toward a standard, not proof that a student has met it.
