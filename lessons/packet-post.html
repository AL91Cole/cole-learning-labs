Ready-to-run lesson · Networking · Grades 4–6

Packet Post

A packet has to travel from the Source to the Library. Your students are the tech team. They rotate path tiles until the route is continuous, send a packet to test it and explain what they saw.

Grades
Grades 4–6
Time
20-minute lesson. About 5 minutes for a first solve. The catalog allows 10 minutes, which leaves room for a replay.
Devices
Built for Chromebook, laptop and tablet browsers, with keyboard or touch. Not yet checked on a managed school Chromebook, so try it on a student device first.
Accounts
None
Play Packet PostOpen the student-mode linkAll lessons

Objective

Students trace a path from a source to a destination and explain why one open link stops delivery.

Words to use: packet (a small piece of data sent across a network), link (a connection between two points), path (the links a packet follows, in order), source and destination.

Materials

  • One device for each student or pair.
  • The lesson link, posted where students can select it. It is in the share box on this page.
  • A shared screen for showing one move, or a four-by-four grid drawn on the board.
  • Paper or sticky notes for the exit question.
  • For the unplugged fallback: one index card and a strip of paper tape.

The network in the game is fictional. Nothing is saved: progress resets when the student leaves the page. There is no time limit or countdown, and students can retry freely. A small "Active on this page" counter sits under the game. It stays on the device and is not a score.

Lesson sequence

The steps add up to 20 minutes.

  1. Ask the opening question (3 min) When you send a message, how does it get to the other device? Collect a few ideas. Introduce the words source, destination, link and path.
  2. Show one move (2 min) Open the game on a shared screen. Point to the entry on the left of Source and the exit on the right of Library. Select one tile to show that it turns a quarter turn. Do not solve the puzzle.
  3. Play (7 min) Students rotate tiles until both ends of every link meet, then select "Send packet" to test the route. If the packet does not arrive, the game says which check to look at, and they try another plan. Early finishers trace the finished path with a finger and count the tiles the packet passes through.
  4. Explain to a partner (4 min) Each student points to one link on the finished path and answers two questions: what would happen if this tile were turned, and why?
  5. Exit question (4 min) Ask the exit question. Close by naming one way the game is simpler than a real network.

What the game models, and what it does not

It does show

  • A packet needs a continuous working path to reach its destination.
  • Every link has to connect at both ends.
  • One open link anywhere on the path stops delivery.
  • Sending a packet is a test: it tells you whether the path works.

It does not show

  • Data split into many labeled pieces and put back together at the destination.
  • Addresses, or how a device knows where to send a packet.
  • Routers choosing a path on their own, or switching to another path when a link fails.
  • Speed, wireless links or a real network map. The grid is a teaching path.

Say one of these differences to students in a sentence, so the puzzle picture does not turn into a wrong idea about real networks. In the game a person turns each tile by hand. Real networks do that work automatically.

Evidence to expect

  • On the screen. A finished route from Source to Library and the game's "Mission complete" message, followed by its own summary: links must meet at both ends, and a packet needs a continuous working path.
  • In partner talk. The game ends with the prompt "Try explaining it: What changed, and why did it work?" Listen for a student who points to a link and explains what it does for the path.
  • At the door. A spoken or written answer to the exit question.

This game has no report to download and keeps nothing after the student leaves the page. Finishing it is practice feedback, not a grade. What you collect is what students say and write.

Look-fors (a concise rubric)

These judge the quality of reasoning, not how fast a student solved the puzzle.

A student may explain aloud to you, or use the device's own district-approved dictation to write the exit answer in the document you already use. The game itself has no boxes to type in. Use the same look-fors either way. The site does not record audio and has no speech input.

Exit question

Your path has seven links. Six connect and one does not. Does the packet arrive? Explain why the place of the open link does or does not matter.

Collect the answer on paper or aloud.

Answer

Unplugged fallback

No devices today? Students become the network. It takes about 15 minutes and needs one index card.

  1. Build the path. Six to eight students stand in a line that bends around the room. Each one is a router. The first is the Source and the last is the Library. Write "packet" on the index card.
  2. Make the links. Each router can pass the card only to the next router in the line, and only when both of them hold out a hand. Two hands that meet are a working link.
  3. Send a packet. The Source passes the card along. The class traces the path aloud as it moves. The packet arrives.
  4. Break one link. Quietly ask one pair of neighbors to keep their hands down, or mark the gap with paper tape on the floor. Send the packet again. It stops. Ask the class: where did it stop, and why can it not go on?
  5. Move the break. Fix that link and break a different one, near the start and then near the end. Ask: does it matter where the open link is?
  6. Close. Ask the exit question. Add one sentence about real networks: routers can often choose another path, and this line cannot.

Students who prefer not to stand in the line can be testers. They predict where the packet will stop before each send.

Standards connection

This lesson lists a concept, not a standard code.

Concept: Networks and Systems Design (Networks and the Internet), grades 4–6, in the New York State Computer Science and Digital Fluency Learning Standards (December 2020).

Why no code is listed: the game asks for one continuous path. It does not yet model data split into labeled pieces, which is what the nearest standard in this concept describes. Naming that standard would claim more than the game shows.

Teacher look-for (our wording, not NYSED's): the student rotates tiles until every link meets at both ends and explains why one open link stops delivery to the Library.

This is the author's own mapping. It is not an official NYSED review or endorsement, and one lesson is practice toward a concept, not proof that a student has mastered it.
