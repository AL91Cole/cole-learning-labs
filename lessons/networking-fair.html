Teaching notes · Networking pathway · Grades 7–12

Run the school tech fair

Students run part of a school tech fair. In each mission a crew makes a decision, sees what happens and uses the evidence to explain it. These notes cover pacing, pair roles, debrief questions and what to look for.

Grades
Grades 7–12, in three bands
Time
Each band is four 40-minute missions and a 40-minute capstone.
Devices
A current browser on a Chromebook, laptop or tablet, with keyboard or touch. Nothing to install. Not yet checked on a school-managed Chromebook, so try one mission on a student device first.
Accounts
None

The shape of every mission

All twelve missions use the same three playable steps, so students learn the routine once.

  1. Get the brief. Students meet the person who needs help, read what they can observe and the measurable target, and predict an outcome by choosing one. A short “why” is optional.
  2. Try it. A worked example models one careful test. Then the crew changes a cable, a place, a setting, a rule or a plan, and runs a test. The reading appears beside the baseline. A miss comes with feedback about that reading.
  3. Prove it. One condition changes. The crew runs a fresh test, selects the evidence it is relying on and tells the next crew to open, hold or revise.

In the lab guide these sit on the site’s usual sections: Introduction, Section 1: Hands-On Demonstration, Section 2: Applied Learning, and Section 3: Challenge and Analysis. The Guided, Independent and Challenge support levels are at the top of the guide.

Three rules the page enforces. Changing a tested setup marks its reading out of date, and an out-of-date reading cannot be selected as evidence. The prediction and the earlier reading stay on screen for comparison. Opening a page or replaying a practice game does not complete a mission: a crew has to test after the change, choose evidence, make a decision that agrees with it and write a handoff.

A decision of hold or revise is a complete answer when the evidence supports it. The station shows as online only when the current evidence meets the target and the crew chose open.

Pacing a 40-minute mission

These times are for The frozen robot cam, the suggested first mission. The other missions fit the same frame.

  1. Launch (5 min) Read the brief aloud. Ask: the robot keeps moving, so what exactly has stopped? Every student chooses a prediction before anyone tests.
  2. Model (5 min) On a shared screen, read the worked example and the baseline row together. Say all three counters aloud, then ask which one is the problem. Do not move the access point yourself.
  3. Pairs (13 min) Crews choose a location, select Run a lap and read all three counters. They keep going until every checkpoint meets the target.
  4. The curtain replay (10 min) Crews select Lower the curtain. Their last reading is now out of date. They run a fresh lap, decide what it means and, if they wish, try another location.
  5. Handoff (7 min) Crews select their evidence, choose open, hold or revise and write one handoff sentence. Two or three crews read theirs aloud.

For the capstone, use 5 minutes to set the scene and choose crews, 25 minutes at the stations and 10 minutes for crews to report which stations opened and why.

Pair roles

  • Driver. Operates the controls. Says each change aloud before making it, and changes one thing at a time.
  • Recorder. Reads every row of the reading aloud, not only the one the crew is hoping for, and keeps the record sheet or the report.
  • Swap at the change. When the condition changes in Prove it, the two swap roles, so both students run a test and both read one.
  • In threes, or on paper. The third student is the card keeper, who holds the evidence cards face down and turns over the card that matches the crew’s setup and test.

Debrief questions

  • What did you predict, and what did the first reading show?
  • Which reading did you nearly skip? What would you have missed?
  • When the condition changed, why could you not keep your earlier result?
  • Which single reading does your decision rest on? What would make you change the decision?
  • Name one thing the next crew should check that you did not.

The habit underneath all of these is the same: say what you saw, say what you tested, and say what you would still check. Entry-level security study asks for that habit too, including the Google Cybersecurity certificate, CompTIA Security+ and a university course in the author’s own degree, CYBR 340. The wording in these missions is original and written for students.

Look-fors

These describe the quality of a crew’s reasoning in four parts of the work. They do not state any mission’s outcome and they are not a grading scheme. Clues and retries carry no penalty, in the missions or here. Keep any point scale and any pass mark in your school’s own private system.

Look forBeginningDevelopingSecure
Defining the problemRepeats the complaint without saying what can be measured.Names what is wrong, but not the target it has to reach.States who needs help, what is observed and the measurable target, in their own words.
Choosing a testChanges several things at once, or tests without a reason.Changes one thing, but cannot say what the test is meant to show.Changes one thing and says beforehand what a pass or a miss would tell them.
Verifying the resultRelies on a reading from before the setup or the condition changed.Runs a fresh test, but reads only the row they expected to improve.Runs a fresh test after every change and reads every row against the target.
Explaining the decisionGives a decision with no reading attached.Points to a reading, but the decision does not follow from it.Gives open, hold or revise, the reading it rests on, and what the next crew should check.

A student may explain aloud, or use the device’s own district-approved dictation in the handoff box. Selecting evidence and using the sentence starter count as explaining.

Evidence to expect

  • On the screen. The station line (offline, testing, target met, on hold or online), the readings table with the baseline and the earlier reading, and the list of every test the crew ran.
  • In the report. My report gathers the prediction, every test with its setup, the changed condition, the selected evidence, the decision and the handoff. Students download it as a text file and hand it in through your approved route.
  • On paper. The mission record sheet in the student handout, filled in from the evidence cards.

A mission and the original investigation it grew from are separate assignments with separate saved work and separate reports. Assigning one never completes the other.

What the missions model, and what they do not

They do show

  • A decision changes a reading, and the reading is the evidence.
  • A result belongs to the setup that produced it.
  • Two promises, such as “scores open” and “staff files blocked”, need two tests.
  • Separate parts do separate jobs: a name lookup and a service, a tunnel and a role, priority and capacity.

They do not show

  • Real radio behaviour. The packet counts are fixed teaching values, not a survey.
  • Real device configuration. No switch, access point, firewall or VPN is set up.
  • Live traffic. Nothing is captured, measured or sent.
  • A full course. Each mission practises one skill from a much larger subject.

Where the ideas come from

The missions are original classroom situations. Their topics follow three university courses the author has studied, used here as a guide to sequence. The missions select a few ideas from each and are not a substitute for any of them.

Access

Every map has a text view and every reading is a table. Controls are ordinary radio buttons, checkboxes and buttons, so a mission can be completed with a keyboard or by touch, with nothing to drag. Status is written in words as well as shown. The site’s Listen and Display tools, including large text, high contrast and reduced motion, apply to mission pages. Print lays a mission out as one document. These were checked in a desktop browser. They have not been checked with a screen reader, on a school-managed device or with a class.
